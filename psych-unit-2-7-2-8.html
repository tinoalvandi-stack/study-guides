Module 2.7 · Forgetting and other memory challenges

Forgetting

Memory is rebuilt each time it is used, not played back like a recording. Forgetting is partly useful: it clears what we no longer need.

Anterograde vs retrograde amnesia

2.7-1 · vocab
Anterograde

Can't form new explicit memories after the injury or illness. Old memories mostly stay.

Brain link: damage to the hippocampus and nearby medial temporal lobe, which turn new experiences into lasting explicit memories. Patients can still learn implicit skills (they improve at a task without remembering practicing it).

Example: 50 First Dates; patient meets the same nurse daily as a stranger.

Retrograde

Can't retrieve memories from before the injury. New learning can still happen.

Often the most recent pre-injury memories are hit hardest. Skills (procedural, implicit) usually survive.

Example: The Bourne Identity; agent keeps his skills, loses his backstory.

Tell: point to the injury on a timeline. Loss going forward (can't add new) = anterograde. Loss going back (can't reach old) = retrograde.

Three ways memory fails

2.7-1
Encoding failure

The info never got in. Unattended details may not be encoded deeply enough for later recall; some information is encoded automatically.

Can't say which colors were on the old Apple logo or how many sides a pencil has. Gets more common with age.

Storage decay

Got in, then faded over time.

Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: steep loss soon after learning new material, then it levels off.

Retrieval failure

Still stored, can't get it out because the right cue is missing.

Tip-of-the-tongue: you know you know it. A cue (first letter) often brings it back.

Tell: was it ever learned? No = encoding. Learned but gone with time = storage decay. A hint brings it back = retrieval.

Proactive vs retroactive interference

2.7-1 · vocab
Proactive

Old info blocks recall of new info. Pro = forward in time.

You keep typing last year's password; Spanish words pop out while you speak French.

Retroactive

New info blocks recall of old info. Retro = backward.

After learning your new locker combo, you can't remember the old one.

Tell: ask "what am I trying to remember?" If you're failing at the new thing, it's proactive. Failing at the old thing, retroactive.

Repression vs suppression (motivated forgetting)

2.7-1 · vocab
Repression

Freud's psychoanalytic idea: the mind blocks anxiety-causing memories without awareness.

Status: a theory, and a disputed one. Many memory researchers think it rarely happens; traumatic events are often remembered too well.

Suppression

Choosing not to think about something painful or distracting. Conscious and deliberate.

Telling yourself "not tonight" about a breakup so you can study for a final.

Tell: unconscious = repression. On purpose = suppression.

Misinformation effect vs source amnesia

2.7-2 · vocab
Misinformation effect

Misleading info after an event changes the memory of the event itself.

Loftus and Palmer: people asked how fast cars "smashed into" each other estimated higher speeds than people asked about "hit."

Source amnesia

The memory may be accurate, but you forget where, when, or how you got it.

You're sure a fact came from the textbook; you actually heard it in a video. Also the basis of many false "I was there" memories.

Tell: is the content of the memory distorted? Misinformation. Is the content fine but the origin wrong? Source amnesia.

Reconsolidation

Pulling a memory out makes it editable; it can change before it gets stored again.

Why retelling a story slowly changes it.

Imagination effect

Vividly imagining an event makes it feel familiar, which can turn it into a false memory.

False memory

A memory that feels real but didn't happen. Feeling confident is not proof.

Piaget "remembered" a kidnapping attempt that a caregiver later admitted inventing.

Real or false? (2.7-2)

Can't tell by vividness or confidence alone. False memories tend to be more general, gist-based; check for outside confirmation.

Kids as eyewitnesses (2.7-3)

Very open to suggestion and leading questions. Most accurate when questioned soon, by a neutral person, with open-ended questions.

Déjà vu

Eerie "I've been here" feeling. Cues in the present unconsciously trigger an old, similar experience. Brain areas tied to it: hippocampus, amygdala, rhinal cortex (familiarity).

Childhood amnesia

Few people remember events from before about age 3. Skills like language stay; specific episodes fade. One leading explanation: rapid new-neuron growth in a still-developing hippocampus.

Use it to study (2.7-4)

Space study; self-test; make it meaningful (own words + example); use cues like the place and mood you'll test in; sleep after studying; limit interference by not cramming similar subjects back to back.

Module 2.6 carryover · retrieval

Getting memories out

On LL3 and needed to explain retrieval failure. May or may not be scored on this quiz.

Recall vs recognition

2.6-1
Recall

Pull the info out with no answer in front of you.

Fill-in-the-blank, no word bank. Harder.

Recognition

Pick out info you learned from options shown.

Multiple choice. Easier, so you "know" more than recall shows.

Relearning

Learning it again goes faster the second time.

Ebbinghaus's measure of what was retained.

Context vs state vs mood effects

2.6-1 · vocab
Context-dependent

Recall is better in the same outside place or situation where you learned.

Asking to sit in the seat where you took notes.

State-dependent

Recall is better in the same inner body or mind state.

Studied on caffeine, tested on caffeine.

Mood-congruent

Your current mood pulls up memories that match it.

Feeling down brings back other bad days.

Tell: a room = context. A body or chemical state = state. Mood choosing which memories come up = mood-congruent. Umbrella: encoding specificity, cues present at learning help most at recall.

Priming

Unconscious activation of linked associations. Seeing "rabbit" makes you more likely to spell "hair" as "hare."

Serial position effect

Best recall for the first (primacy) and last (recency) items on a list; the middle drops out.

Testing effect, interleaving, metacognition (2.6-2)

Self-quizzing strengthens recall. Interleaving mixes topics in one session. Metacognition = checking what you actually know vs what feels familiar.

Module 2.8a · theories of intelligence

One intelligence or many?

Intelligence: ability to learn from experience, solve problems, and use knowledge to adapt to new situations (2.8-1).

Charles SpearmanGeneral intelligence (g)One general ability underlies every mental task, plus specific abilities (s). Used factor analysis: stats that find clusters of related test items. Argument for g: scores on different kinds of tests correlate (2.8-2).
L. L. Thurstone7 primary mental abilitiesSplit intelligence into seven clusters (e.g., reasoning, word fluency, spatial, numerical, memory). Catch: people strong in one tended to be strong in the others, which supported g.
Raymond CattellFluid (Gf) + crystallized (Gc)Two kinds of ability that work together. See the comparison below.
Cattell-Horn-CarrollCHC theoryThree layers: general intelligence (g) on top; broad abilities in the middle, including Gf and Gc among others; many narrow abilities at the bottom. A model, not settled fact.
Howard GardnerMultiple intelligences (8, possible 9th)Linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, intrapersonal, interpersonal, naturalist. Existential was proposed as a possible ninth, not formally added. Support: savants. Criticism: some are talents, not intelligences; g still predicts success.
Robert SternbergTriarchic: analytical, creative, practicalAnalytical = academic problem solving (what tests measure). Creative = new ideas. Practical = everyday "street smarts." Criticism: the three overlap and correlate with g.

Gardner vs Sternberg (2.8-4)

2.8-4
Differ

Gardner: 8 fairly independent intelligences (existential proposed as a possible 9th), broad (music, body, nature). Sternberg: 3, focused on success in life.

Agree

Multiple abilities contribute to life success. Different kinds of giftedness add variety to life and challenges for education.

Shared criticism

A general factor (g) still shows up and predicts school and job performance across many tasks.

Emotional intelligence (2.8-5)

Four parts: perceive emotions (read faces), understand them (predict how they change), manage them (express fittingly), use them (to adapt or create).

Social intelligence

Know-how for reading social situations and handling yourself well in them.

Grit

Passion plus perseverance toward long-term goals. Predicts achievement beyond raw ability.

Savant syndrome

An exceptional narrow skill (calendar math, drawing, music) that stands out against the person's overall functioning. Low general intelligence is not required. Often occurs alongside developmental or neurological conditions: many are autistic, but most autistic people are not savants. Most are male. Supports Gardner's separate-intelligences idea.

Fluid vs crystallized

2.8-3 · 2.8c
Fluid (Gf)

Reason quickly and abstractly on new problems, independent of what you've learned.

Age: tends to decline in adulthood, especially late adulthood. Timing varies by person and task.

Solving a puzzle you've never seen.

Crystallized (Gc)

Accumulated knowledge and verbal skills.

Age: tends to increase or hold into later life.

Vocabulary, knowing how a map works.

Tell: new problem, no prior knowledge needed = fluid. Uses what you already know = crystallized. They work together.

Module 2.8b · assessing intelligence

Testing and IQ

Intelligence test: measures mental aptitudes and compares them to others with numerical scores.

Aptitude vs achievement

2.8-6 · vocab
Aptitude

Predicts future performance; capacity to learn.

SAT/ACT used to predict college success.

Achievement

Measures what you've already learned.

AP exam in May, a unit test.

Tell: looks forward = aptitude. Looks back = achievement. The line blurs in real tests.

late 1800s

Francis Galton: tried to measure "natural" ability; founded eugenics (restricting reproduction of people judged unfit). Discredited and harmful.

early 1900s

Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon (France): built a test to find kids who needed extra school help. Idea of mental age: the level typical of a given age.

1910s

William Stern: ratio IQ = mental age ÷ chronological age × 100. Lewis Terman (Stanford): revised Binet's test into the Stanford-Binet for U.S. use.

1950s on

David Wechsler: WAIS (adults) and WISC (kids). Most used today. Verbal and nonverbal (performance) subtests; gives an overall score plus part scores.

Historical ratio IQ vs modern normed IQ (2.8-7)

2.8-7
Then: ratio IQ

IQ = MA ÷ CA × 100.

A 10-year-old who performs like a typical 12-year-old: 12 ÷ 10 × 100 = 120. Breaks down for adults (mental age stops climbing).

Now: normed score

Your score is compared to others your age in a standardization sample. Average is set at 100; scores fall on a normal curve.

No mental age division. Works at any age.

Psychometrics

The science of measuring abilities, attitudes, and traits.

Standardization

Same procedures for everyone, and scores given meaning by comparing to a pretested group.

Normal curve

Symmetrical bell shape. Most scores near average, fewer at the extremes.

Flynn effect

Average IQ test performance rose over the 20th century across many countries, so tests get re-normed. Too fast to be genetic; likely nutrition, schooling, environment.

Reliability vs validity (2.8-8)

2.8-8 · vocab
Reliability = consistent

Test-retest: same people, same test, later; similar scores.

Split-half: split one test into two equivalent halves (e.g., odd vs even items) and correlate each person's two half-scores.

Alternate forms: two versions of the test give similar scores.

Validity = measures the right thing

Content: samples the behavior it's about (a driving test that has you drive).

Predictive: scores predict the future behavior it's meant to (entrance exam scores track first-year college GPA).

Construct: measures the underlying trait it claims to (an intelligence test that actually taps intelligence, not just reading speed).

Tell: a thermometer that reads 10° too hot every time is reliable but not valid. A test can't be valid if it isn't reliable.

Module 2.8c · stability and influences

Age, genes, and mindset

Cross-sectional vs longitudinal (and cohort)

2.8c · Unit 0 tie-in
Cross-sectional

Compare different age groups at one time.

Fast. Problem: groups grew up in different eras, so cohort differences (schooling, tech) can look like age effects. Early cross-sectional work made aging look like steep decline.

Longitudinal

Follow the same people and retest them over years.

Slow, people drop out. Showed intelligence stays fairly stable until late in life.

Cohort

A group sharing a trait, usually birth era.

The confound cross-sectional studies can't separate from age.

Heritability

The share of differences among people in a group that can be linked to genes. It's a group statistic, not a measure of one person, and it changes with the environment studied.

Evidence: twin and adoption studies. Adopted kids' scores resemble adoptive parents less as they age. Genes are not destiny; environment (schooling, nutrition) still moves scores.

Growth vs fixed mindset

Growth: abilities improve with effort and learning. Fixed: abilities are set and unchangeable.

Growth mindset links to persistence after setbacks.

Aging overall

One early view: ability declines with age. Longitudinal data: fairly stable until late life; Gc holds, Gf slips.

Module 2.8d · group differences and bias

Bias and stereotype threat

Group averages differ on some tests, but individual variation within groups is far larger than differences between them, and environment explains much of the gap.

Test bias vs stereotype threat

2.8d · vocab
Test bias

Two senses: (1) cultural: the test assumes experiences some groups lack (unfamiliar references, language). (2) Statistical: the test predicts outcomes less accurately for one group. Many major tests predict about equally well across groups, while still reflecting unequal opportunity.

Stereotype threat

Worry, in the moment, that you'll be judged by a negative stereotype about your group, which lowers performance.

Steele and Aronson: Black students scored lower when a test was framed as measuring intellectual ability; without that framing, the gap shrank. You don't have to believe the stereotype; knowing it applies to the situation is enough.

Tell: problem lives in the test = bias. Problem lives in the testing situation (pressure created by a stereotype) = stereotype threat.

Short FRQ 1: Compare proactive and retroactive interference using one student learning two languages.
  1. Proactive: older learning blocks newer learning. After years of Spanish, a student starting French says the Spanish word for "library" when trying to recall the French one.
  2. Retroactive: newer learning blocks older learning. After a semester of French, the same student struggles to recall Spanish vocabulary.
  3. Key contrast: which memory is being lost. Losing the new one = proactive; losing the old one = retroactive.
Short FRQ 2: Explain reliability and predictive validity using a new college entrance exam.
  1. Reliability: the exam gives consistent results. Students retaking it a month later get similar scores (test-retest), or each student's odd-item and even-item halves agree (split-half).
  2. Predictive validity: scores predict what the exam is meant to predict. Higher scorers earn higher first-year college GPAs.
  3. Link: an exam can be reliable without being valid (consistent scores that don't predict GPA), but it can't be valid if it isn't reliable.
Watch out: common mix-ups
  • Anterograde amnesia: "antero" means forward in time, not the front of the brain. The classic cause is hippocampal / medial temporal lobe damage (patient H.M.), with new explicit memory lost and implicit learning spared.
  • Module numbering: this guide uses the LL3 numbering, 2.7 = forgetting and 2.8a to 2.8d = intelligence.
  • Fluid intelligence: it tends to decrease with age, especially in late adulthood. It does not increase with age, and no single peak age (such as the early 20s) applies to everyone.
  • Split-half reliability: compares each person's scores on two equivalent halves of one test. It is not groups swapping written and multiple-choice days.
  • Stereotype threat: a self-confirming concern about being judged. It works without believing (internalizing) the stereotype.
  • Repression: Freud's theory, and a disputed one; trauma is often remembered vividly. Don't treat it as established fact.
  • IQ formula: MA ÷ CA × 100 is the historical ratio IQ, credited to William Stern. Modern tests score you against same-age norms.
  • Heritability: a group statistic about variation, not one person's fate. A single adoption anecdote is not evidence for it.
  • Memory stages: sensory, short-term, long-term (sensory, not "somatic").
