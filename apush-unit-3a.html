Republicanism and the new states

American republicanism: three definitions, all three count

AdamsHamiltonPaine
IdeaNatural aristocracy: talented people rise to lead, open to anyone with talent and drive, not birth or wealthRational pursuit of economic self-interest: getting rich is fine as long as it does not harm the republicEgalitarianism: open up the political process, for example by lowering property qualifications for voting
DutyLeaders sacrifice for the good of the nationWealth builds the nationMore people take part
Pushed byEducated eliteEducated and economic eliteCommon people

A democracy is rule by the majority. A republic is government restrained by a fixed body of law. The U.S. is a republic with democratic elements, such as voting.

Slavery after the Revolution

  • The North ends slavery state by state: some through their state constitutions, others through gradual emancipation (children born after a set date are free).
  • In the South, slavery hardens. To square it with "all men are created equal," southerners develop racist theories of African inferiority.
  • Result: a free North and a slave South, split roughly at the Ohio River.

The new state governments

  • Colonists distrusted governors (the king's stand-in), so states made weak governors and powerful legislatures.
  • States wrote their own bills of rights and lowered property qualifications for voting.

Articles of Confederation (1781 to 1789) compare with the Constitution

  • The first national government, replacing the Second Continental Congress. It took effect in 1781, after Virginia and the other big states gave up their claims to western land, which won over the last holdout, Maryland.
  • The states kept enormous sovereignty; the Articles government had only what the states allowed it.
  • Structure: a unicameral (one-house) Congress, one state, one vote (Rhode Island equal to Virginia), no executive (no president), no national courts. No separation of powers.

Powers on paper, mostly unusable

  • Conduct foreign policy
  • Settle disputes between states
  • Regulate international trade
  • Levy taxes on the states

What actually worked

  • Setting the value of coins (their gold and silver content)
  • Standard weights and measures, to ease trade
  • The Northwest Ordinances, its greatest success

Weaknesses

  • No power over interstate trade: states put tariffs on each other's goods.
  • Could levy taxes but not collect them: the government was "reduced to begging" the states.
  • No army, so no enforcement mechanism.
  • Could not settle disputes between states: Maryland and Virginia settled their Potomac River dispute themselves at Mount Vernon (1785).
  • Foreign nations ignored it and dealt with individual states.
  • Amendments needed all 13 states (unanimity), so it could not be fixed.

Northwest Ordinances memorize

1784: Jefferson's plan

  • Territories would join the Union as equal states, not colonies.
  • To apply: population of the smallest state, pay taxes, never leave the Union, have a republican government.
  • Its slavery ban failed by one vote. Replaced by the 1785 and 1787 ordinances.

1785: Land Ordinance

  • Land is surveyed before it is sold.
  • Township = 6 miles by 6 miles = 36 sections. Section = 1 square mile = 640 acres.
  • Sold in whole sections; land speculators bought them and subdivided them for small farmers.
  • Sale of the 16th section in each township funds public education, because a republic needs informed voters.

1787: Northwest Ordinance, the Add-a-State Plan

StageTriggerWhat happens
1Few or no settlersCongress appoints 1 governor, 3 judges, 1 secretary
25,000 adult free malesSettlers elect a territorial legislature that makes laws for the territory
360,000 people (enslaved people not counted)Write a state constitution, residents approve it, Congress admits the state as an equal of the original 13
  • The Northwest Territory becomes 3 to 5 states. Settlers get a bill of rights.
  • Slavery is prohibited in the Northwest Territory. The South accepted it because the region had no tobacco or cotton economy.
  • The same path to statehood is still used today.
Trap: 5,000 adult free males gets a legislature; 60,000 people gets statehood. The slavery ban failed in 1784 and passed in 1787.

Postwar crisis and Shays' Rebellion

Depression, 1781 to 1787

  • War production ended; farm prices fell; farmers lost land to foreclosure.
  • Outside the British Empire, Americans faced British tariffs and lost old markets.
  • No national currency: paper money circulated at unstable values.
  • States placed tariffs on other states' goods, acting like separate countries.

Shays' Rebellion (1786)

  • Western Massachusetts farmers led by Daniel Shays revolted against state taxes, foreclosures and debt imprisonment.
  • They argued the western counties were underrepresented in the legislature, so the taxes were unfair.
  • The state was too weak and broke; eastern bankers and merchants paid for the militia that put it down.
  • Significance: proof the Articles could not keep order. See the comparison with the Whiskey Rebellion below.

Writing the Constitution (1787)

  • Hamilton called for a stronger central government from the start.
  • Annapolis Convention (1786): met on trade, but only 5 states came. Hamilton used it to call for a convention to amend the Articles.
  • Constitutional Convention (1787, Philadelphia): 12 of 13 states (Rhode Island refused). Wealthy, educated delegates; Washington presided; Madison is the "father of the Constitution." It met in secret and wrote a new government instead of amending the old one.
  • The two problems: build a government strong enough to fix the Articles, while protecting the people's rights from tyranny.

Three-Fifths Compromise

  • The South wanted enslaved people counted for House seats but not direct taxes; the North wanted the opposite.
  • Deal: three-fifths of the enslaved population counts for both. Free population + 3/5 of enslaved population = the state's number.
  • Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved people until 1808, part of an unspoken agreement to stay silent on slavery.

Commercial (regulation of trade) Compromise

  • The federal government regulates foreign trade (tariffs on imports) and interstate trade.
  • It does not regulate trade within a state.
  • It can never tax exports (profits can be taxed, exports cannot).
Trap: the Virginia Plan had both houses by population; the Constitution did not. And the Three-Fifths Compromise was a counting rule for seats and taxes, not a statement that a person is three-fifths of a human being.

Key checks and balances

  • President can veto a bill; Congress can override with two-thirds of each house.
  • President nominates judges and officials; the Senate confirms.
  • President signs treaties; the Senate ratifies by two-thirds; courts can rule a treaty unconstitutional.
  • Impeachment: the House accuses (bribery, treason, high crimes and misdemeanors) by majority vote; the Senate holds the trial; two-thirds of senators to convict and remove. Impeached does not mean removed.
  • Courts can rule laws and executive orders unconstitutional.

The president serves 4-year terms; Washington set the two-term tradition. The vice president presides over the Senate and breaks ties.

Ratification (1787 to 1788)

  • Decided by elected state ratifying conventions, not the Articles Congress; 9 of 13 states needed.
  • Federalists supported ratification; Anti-Federalists opposed it as too much central power. These were viewpoints, not parties yet.
  • The Federalist Papers (Hamilton, Madison, Jay) argued for ratification in New York.
  • Several states, Massachusetts first, ratified on the promise that the first Congress would add a Bill of Rights. No need to know which states or the vote counts.

First Congress and Washington

  • Revenue Act of 1789: a small tariff on imported goods, the federal government's first independent source of income, something the Articles never had.
  • Bill of Rights: the first ten amendments, ratified in 1791; rights the government cannot take away. The 10th leaves other powers to the states and the people.
  • Congress creates the executive departments: State (Jefferson), Treasury (Hamilton), War (Knox), plus an Attorney General. The president can dismiss the officials he appoints.
  • The cabinet is not in the Constitution: it is a precedent Washington set by using department heads as his advisers. The vice president was not part of it. Washington also set the "Mr. President" title and the two-term tradition.
  • Judiciary Act of 1789: filled in the short Article III with three levels of federal courts, district courts, circuit courts of appeals and the Supreme Court at the top.
  • 1790 Quaker petition to end the slave trade: Congress would hear antislavery petitions but could not end the foreign slave trade before 1808; slavery stayed a state matter.

Hamilton's financial plan core review

The goal

  • Every piece of the plan pulled power to the national level and built a wealthy investor class tied to the new government.
  • Hamiltonian republicanism: people are driven by the rational pursuit of economic self-interest, so tie their money to the nation's survival.

Funding the federal debt

  • Pay the existing federal debt in full, through new federal bonds.
  • Paid whoever held the bond now, mostly speculators who bought at about half price.
  • Madison wanted repayment shared between the original holders (soldiers, small farmers) and the current holders. Rejected as unworkable.

Assumption of state debts

  • Federal government takes over the states' debts and folds them into the national debt.
  • Old state bonds exchanged for new federal securities; now the federal government owed the holders.
  • Logic: "if I hold your debt, I hold power over you." Creditors now need the federal government to last.
  • Virginia had already paid off much of its debt and objected to covering other states' debts. South Carolina, still heavily in debt, favored assumption. The South was not united.

Bonds and speculators in one line

A bond is a government IOU (face value, maturity date, interest rate) that only works if people believe it will be paid. Desperate holders sold cheap; speculators, including some members of Congress, bought, betting on full repayment. Assumption paid off that bet.

Compromise of 1790 (the capital deal)

Worked out at a dinner Jefferson hosted: Madison and Virginia's allies stop blocking assumption and enough southern votes switch; in return the national capital goes south, on land given up by Virginia and Maryland. Result: Washington, D.C.

Bank of the United States (1791)

  • A privately managed bank with federal backing. The federal government subscribed about one-fifth (20%) of the stock; the rest was private. All 25 directors were elected each year by the stockholders. A big shareholder, not the manager.
  • Investors could buy Bank stock with their new federal bonds from assumption.
  • Jobs: holds federal money, lends to the government, issues banknotes that were widely accepted as money (not an exclusive government currency), works with state banks.
  • 20-year charter; Congress refused to renew it in 1811.
Trap: not the modern Federal Reserve, and not entirely government-owned. Some notes say the government named one-fifth of the directors; under the 1791 charter the stockholders elected all 25, with the government voting its one-fifth share. Either way: influence, not control.

Strict vs. broad construction

Strict: Jefferson, MadisonBroad (loose): Hamilton
Bank isUnconstitutionalConstitutional
Reading of the Necessary and Proper (Elastic) ClauseOnly what is truly necessary to carry out an enumerated power, not what is merely convenient or desirable. The government can function without a bank.Implied powers: if the end is constitutional (taxing, borrowing, regulating commerce) and the means is not prohibited, the means is constitutional.
OutcomeWashington accepts Hamilton's argument and signs the bill.
Trap: broad construction is not "anything not forbidden is allowed." The means must connect to a power the Constitution actually lists (Article I, Section 8).

Raising revenue: tariff vs. excise

  • Tariff = tax on imports. Hamilton's Report on Manufactures proposed tariffs and subsidies (bounties) to grow American factories. Congress did not adopt the full program, especially the subsidies, which many saw as too much federal power, but it passed most of the report's tariff recommendations in 1792.
  • Excise tax = tax on a specific good made at home. The whiskey tax was an excise, paid by distillers.
Trap: the Whiskey Rebellion was over an excise tax, not a tariff. And the course outline's shorthand "rejected by Congress" for the Report on Manufactures means the overall industrial program, not every tariff in it.

Whiskey Rebellion heavily stressed

  • Western Pennsylvania farmers turned corn into whiskey because it was cheaper to ship east. The federal excise fell right on them.
  • Resistance from 1791, peak in 1794: attacks on federal marshals; a tax inspector's house burned.
  • Washington raised about 13,000 militia from four states and personally led them west. No battle; rebels scattered.
  • Two men convicted of treason, sentenced to death, then pardoned by Washington.
  • Point: you may protest or petition a law, but you may not stop its enforcement. The Constitution's government is not weak like the Articles.

First party system (1791)

Born from the Bank fight. The real issue: how much power the federal government should have. Both sides accepted the Supremacy Clause; they disagreed on degree. The Constitution never planned for parties.

Trap: the Anti-Federalists were not a party; they opposed ratifying the Constitution in 1787 to 1788. Many later drifted to the Democratic-Republicans. And Jefferson's Republicans are not the modern Republican Party (founded 1854). Washington belonged to no party but usually sided with the Federalists.

Foreign policy under Washington

French Revolution and Citizen Genêt (1793)

  • Americans first cheered the Revolution, then recoiled at the Terror. Many saw France as a perversion of republicanism.
  • The 1778 Franco-American Alliance was still on paper when France and Britain went to war. The U.S. was not ready to fight Britain again.
  • Edmond Genêt, France's diplomat, broke the basic rule of diplomacy: he appealed directly to the American public and handed out privateering commissions to attack British shipping.
  • This happened under Washington, with Jefferson as Secretary of State. Even Jefferson found Genêt outrageous.
  • Washington declared the U.S. impartial (neutral) in the war. Genêt later got asylum and stayed in America.

Trap: Jay did not end impressment. The outline's shorthand says the treaty made no mention of maritime rights; more precisely, it dealt with ship-seizure claims and trade but left impressment and neutral rights unresolved. Right of deposit at New Orleans is Pinckney, not Jay. Hamilton quietly told the British the U.S. would not go to war, which weakened Jay's bargaining.

Washington's Farewell Address (1796)

  • Washington declined a third term. The address was printed in a newspaper, never delivered aloud; Hamilton helped write it.
  • Avoid permanent alliances and entangling European military alliances. Trade with everyone, but keep political ties few. Foundation of U.S. foreign policy until after World War II.
  • Warned against party spirit (a jab at the Democratic-Republicans) and sectional division.
Trap: it did not ban foreign trade.

Adams and the crisis of 1798

Election of 1796

Old rules (before the 12th Amendment): each elector cast two votes; the leader became president if named by a majority of electors, and the runner-up became vice president. Result: Adams president, Jefferson vice president, rivals from opposing parties. Peaceful transfer from Washington.

XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War

  • Adams sent three envoys (one was C. C. Pinckney) to Paris. Three agents of French foreign minister Talleyrand (code names X, Y, Z) demanded a bribe, a large loan, and an apology from Adams.
  • Adams released the papers. Public fury: "Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute." Federalists rode the anti-French wave.
  • Quasi-War (1798 to 1800): an undeclared naval war with France, ship raids in the Caribbean, no declaration by Congress. Adams avoided full war; the Convention of 1800 ended it and the 1778 alliance.

The four Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)

LawWhat it didAimed at
Naturalization ActResidency for citizenship from 5 to 14 yearsImmigrants, who tended to vote Democratic-Republican

  • The Sedition Act clashed with the First Amendment, but the Supreme Court never struck it down; it simply expired in 1801.
Trap: the Sedition Act did not make every criticism illegal, and it was not only for editors (it covered speech too, and other critics were charged). Its targets were mostly Democratic-Republican newspaper editors.

Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798)

KentuckyVirginia
AuthorJefferson (secretly; he was vice president)Madison (secretly)
Shared protestThe Alien and Sedition Acts are unconstitutional; the states, as parties to the Constitution, may judge when Congress oversteps its granted powers.
Remedy wording1798: the acts are void. 1799 follow-up: nullification is the "rightful remedy."States should interpose to stop the harm; the form left open. More cautious.
  • An influential early states' rights argument, and the root of nullification: a state declaring a federal law it judges unconstitutional void and not binding within its borders.
  • Arguing that a law is unconstitutional does not by itself defy federal supremacy. A state unilaterally nullifying a valid federal law does clash with the Supremacy Clause (Article VI): the Constitution, federal laws made under it, and treaties are the supreme law of the land.
  • A contested claim, not settled law: no other state endorsed the resolutions, and most said courts, not states, should judge constitutionality.
  • Looks ahead to the Nullification Crisis (South Carolina and the tariffs, Unit 4).
Trap: this is states judging federal law, not judicial review (the Supreme Court judging laws, established later in Marbury v. Madison).

Election of 1800

  • The "Revolution of 1800": the first time power passed from one party to another, Federalists to Democratic-Republicans, and it happened peacefully.
  • Under the old rules, Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied. A tie goes to the House of Representatives, one vote per state.
  • Hamilton backed Jefferson (wrong on policy but honest) over Burr, deepening the Hamilton and Burr feud.
  • Jefferson kept Hamilton's Bank. The problems of 1796 and 1800 led to the 12th Amendment (separate electoral votes for president and vice president).

Common MCQ traps

Virginia Plan = both houses by population. The Constitution = House by population, Senate equal.
Three-Fifths was a counting rule for House seats and direct taxes, not a definition of a person.
5,000 adult free males = territorial legislature. 60,000 people = statehood.
Washington presided over the Convention in 1787; he became president in 1789.
Federalists of 1787 backed ratification; the Federalist Party of the 1790s was Hamilton and Adams's party.
Annapolis (1786, 5 states, failed) is not Philadelphia (1787, 12 states, the Constitution).
Genêt was 1793, under Washington, with Jefferson as Secretary of State. Not under Adams.
Whiskey tax = excise. Report on Manufactures = tariffs and subsidies for industry (full program not adopted; many tariff ideas passed in 1792).
Madison wanted original bondholders paid. That idea lost.
Jay = western forts, no war, no fix for impressment. Pinckney = Mississippi, New Orleans, 31st parallel.
1796 = Adams and Jefferson from opposite parties. The tie and the House vote are 1800.
Quasi-War was never declared.
Sedition Act expired in 1801; the Supreme Court did not strike it down.
Kentucky and Virginia were state resolutions, not court rulings, and other states rejected them.
Anti-Federalists (ratification fight) are not the Democratic-Republicans (party of the 1790s).
Two people named Pinckney: Thomas (treaty with Spain, 1796 Federalist candidate) and C. C. (XYZ envoy, 1800 candidate). If unsure, "Pinckney" alone is enough.
